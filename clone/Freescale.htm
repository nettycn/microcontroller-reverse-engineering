Brand Overview

Freescale Semiconductor was formed from Motorola's semiconductor division and later acquired by NXP. They are famous for their robust automotive microcontrollers, including the S12 and MPC families, as well as the ColdFire series (a 32‑bit architecture based on the 68k) and the Kinetis series (ARM Cortex‑M) with advanced security features. Freescale chips are widely used in automotive ECUs, industrial control, avionics, networking, and IoT, and have a reputation for high reliability and long product life cycles.

Their microcontroller portfolio covers 8‑bit (S08), 16‑bit (S12/X), and 32‑bit (ColdFire, Kinetis, i.MX) architectures. Many Freescale devices incorporate hardware encryption and secure boot, and they offer flash protection, secure debug, and tamper detection. Many legacy systems still rely on Freescale parts, making reverse engineering crucial for maintenance and upgrade. We have extensive experience with all Freescale families, including custom ASICs, and we can extract firmware from locked S12 and S08 devices, as well as recover encrypted data from Kinetis secure modules.

Our team has successfully cloned Freescale MCUs for automotive and medical clients, covering both invasive and non‑invasive approaches. We provide complete solutions for legacy system maintenance, re‑engineering to migrate Freescale code to modern platforms, and we ensure confidentiality throughout the project. With over 300 Freescale‑based products under our belt and a success rate exceeding 95%, we are your trusted partner for Freescale reverse engineering.

Security Features & Reverse Engineering Insights

Freescale MCUs incorporate multiple layers of security to protect firmware and data. The flash memory can be protected via the FPROT register, which prevents reading or writing, and many devices have a security byte that, when set, disables external access via the background debug module (BDM). However, we have developed techniques to bypass these protections using glitch attacks – by manipulating the voltage or clock, we can force the MCU to skip the security check, allowing us to read back the entire flash content without the need for a password. For S12 devices, we can exploit the unsecured mode during reset to dump the memory, and we have also used fault injection to corrupt the security byte and unlock the device.

ColdFire MCUs feature a security lock that prevents flash readout via JTAG; we can attack this by decapping the chip and using micro‑probing to read the flash directly. For Kinetis ARM MCUs, NXP provides a robust RDP (Read‑out Protection) scheme with levels 0, 1, and 2, but none are completely secure. We have successfully extracted firmware from Kinetis devices at RDP level 2 by using side‑channel analysis to recover the encryption key, then decrypting the flash image. We also provide secure data recovery from the FlexNVM and EEPROM partitions, and we have hacked the secure boot mechanism by replacing the public key with our own, enabling us to load custom firmware on locked devices.

We can duplicate the complete device by reading all memory regions, including calibration data and unique trim values stored in OTP. Our team has reverse‑engineered the proprietary debug protocols used by Freescale, allowing us to re‑enable debugging even when it is disabled in software. For automotive ECUs, we often encounter encryption on the CAN bus; we can extract the encryption keys from the MCU to clone the ECU. We have successfully cloned multiple Freescale‑based ECUs for aftermarket applications, providing both firmware extraction and full system analysis.

Our services include detailed reports on all security weaknesses found, and we offer hardening recommendations to improve protection. Our experience covers all generations from 8‑bit to 32‑bit, and we have developed custom tools to automate the extraction process, significantly reducing time and cost. With a proven track record in the automotive industry and strict confidentiality, MikaTech is ready to tackle any Freescale challenge.
