About Altera

Altera Corporation, now a subsidiary of Intel (Intel Programmable Solutions Group), is a leading manufacturer of programmable logic devices. Founded in 1983, Altera pioneered the CMOS programmable logic array and later introduced the first reprogrammable CPLDs and FPGAs. Their devices are used in a wide range of applications, from consumer electronics to telecommunications, automotive, military, and data centers. Altera's product lines include the high-end Stratix series, mid-range Arria, and low-cost Cyclone families, as well as the MAX CPLD series. In 2015, Intel acquired Altera to integrate FPGAs with its processors, creating new heterogeneous computing platforms. Altera FPGAs are SRAM-based, meaning they lose configuration on power-off and require an external configuration memory (often a serial flash or EEPROM). This configuration bitstream can be encrypted with AES-256 to protect the intellectual property. Altera also offers secure device features like JTAG authentication and tamper protection. The development environment, Quartus Prime, supports a wide range of IP cores and design methodologies. Altera devices are known for their high performance, high density, and advanced transceiver capabilities. The company has also introduced hardened ARM Cortex-A9 and A53 processors in its SoC FPGAs, combining software programmability with hardware acceleration. Altera's FPGAs are widely used in 5G infrastructure, industrial automation, and aerospace. The company has a strong commitment to quality and reliability, with many devices qualified for automotive (AEC-Q100) and military specifications. Altera also provides a comprehensive security suite, including secure boot, physical unclonable functions (PUFs), and side-channel attack countermeasures. Despite these protections, reverse engineering Altera devices is a common requirement for legacy system recovery, IP verification, and competitive analysis. Our team has extensive experience in extracting configuration bitstreams from both volatile SRAM and external configuration memories. We have successfully recovered designs from hundreds of Altera FPGAs, including those with encryption enabled. The process involves understanding the configuration protocol, exploiting vulnerabilities in the configuration interface, or directly reading the external memory. For devices with readback disabled, we employ invasive techniques such as probing the configuration pins during power-up. Altera's security features have evolved over time, but we stay updated with the latest attack vectors. We also provide services to clone Altera FPGAs by replicating the configuration data into a new device. Our lab is equipped with high-speed logic analyzers, FPGA development boards, and custom software tools to automate the extraction process. We have successfully attacked both the older MAX 7000 series and the latest Stratix 10 devices. Altera's bitstream encryption is robust, but we have developed methods to recover the key through side-channel analysis, specifically by monitoring the power supply during decryption. We also use fault injection to cause misconfiguration and bypass security locks. Our goal is to provide a reliable and efficient service, ensuring that you can recover your valuable design data even when the original source files are lost. With our expertise, you can decrypt, read, dump, and even clone Altera FPGAs with high success rates. We understand the nuances of each family, from the low-cost Cyclone IV to the high-end Arria 10. Our approach combines electrical characterization, timing analysis, and physical inspection to overcome any obstacle. We have also developed proprietary algorithms to reconstruct the netlist from the raw bitstream, enabling you to understand the internal logic. Whether you need to recover a lost configuration, duplicate a production device, or analyze a competitor's product, MikaTech is your trusted partner for Altera reverse engineering.

Security Features and Attack Vectors

Altera FPGAs incorporate a range of security features to protect the configuration bitstream from unauthorized access. The most prominent is the AES-256 encryption of the configuration data, which can be stored in an external memory device. The encryption key is stored in the FPGA's internal non-volatile memory (eFuse or battery-backed RAM) and is never exposed externally. Altera also supports a secure configuration mode where the FPGA verifies the authenticity of the bitstream using an HMAC (hash-based message authentication code). This ensures that only authorized configurations are loaded, preventing malicious insertion. Additionally, the JTAG interface can be permanently disabled by blowing a security fuse, blocking any readback or reprogramming attempts. Some devices also feature a "voltage tamper" detection that erases the configuration upon sensing a glitch. Altera's newer families include a physically unclonable function (PUF) that generates a unique device fingerprint, used for secure key generation. However, no system is completely immune to attack. Our reverse engineering team has developed effective methods to bypass these protections. For instance, we can use side-channel power analysis to extract the AES key during the decryption process, as the device consumes different amounts of power depending on the key and data. By capturing many traces and applying differential power analysis (DPA), we can recover the key without ever accessing the internal storage. Another approach is to glitch the power supply or clock signal to disrupt the security state machine, unlocking the JTAG interface for a short window. This allows us to read the configuration data directly from the SRAM cells. For devices with readback disabled, we can still recover the bitstream by eavesdropping on the configuration pins (DATA, DCLK, nCONFIG) during power-up, as the configuration is loaded from an external flash. We have successfully captured the entire bitstream in this manner, provided the encryption is not used. If encryption is active, we resort to decapsulating the device and using FIB to probe the internal data buses. This is an advanced technique that requires precision, but we have successfully recovered encrypted keys from Stratix and Arria devices. We also use electromagnetic (EM) analysis to capture the emissions generated during the configuration process, which can reveal the bitstream content. Our team has developed custom hardware to synchronize with the configuration clock and sample the data lines accurately. For older devices like MAX CPLDs, which use EEPROM or flash technology, we can simply read the memory contents using standard programmers, unless the security fuse is blown. In that case, we apply voltage stress or UV light to reset the fuse, as we have done with many EEPROM-based CPLDs. Our lab is equipped with a wide range of tools, including high-end oscilloscopes, logic analyzers, and specialized programmers for Altera devices. We have also created software tools that can parse and interpret the raw bitstream, converting it into a human-readable netlist or even a fully functional clone. This enables our clients to recover their designs, update obsolete products, or perform competitive analysis. Our success rate with Altera devices is exceptional, and we have successfully attacked even the most recent 14nm and 20nm families. We are committed to staying at the forefront of FPGA security research, ensuring that we can always provide a solution for your reverse engineering needs.
