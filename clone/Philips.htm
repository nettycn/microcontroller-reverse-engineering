About Philips

Philips Semiconductors was a pioneer in the microcontroller industry, famous for its 80C51 family and later LPC ARM series. Although Philips divested its semiconductor division (now NXP), many legacy Philips MCUs are still in service worldwide. Their 80C51 devices were widely used in consumer electronics, automotive, and industrial controls. Philips also produced the LPC2000 series, one of the first ARM7 MCUs. They were known for reliable performance and extensive peripheral integration. Philips’ microcontrollers included on‑chip flash, EEPROM, and multiple timers. They offered low‑power variants for battery‑operated devices. Philips also developed secure smart card ICs. Their products were characterized by long product lifecycles. Many engineers grew up programming Philips 8051 devices. Philips provided comprehensive documentation and application notes. They were a major supplier for the European automotive industry. Philips also produced I²C devices, which were co‑invented by them. Their microcontrollers often featured multiple communication interfaces. Philips supported both assembly and C development. They had a strong presence in medical equipment and industrial automation. Philips’ legacy chips are still used in critical infrastructure. The company’s focus on quality ensured high reliability. Even after the spin‑off, many Philips part numbers remain available through NXP. Reverse engineering of these devices is crucial for maintaining legacy systems. Philips’ architecture is well‑understood and widely documented. They also offered mask ROM versions for high‑volume production. Philips’ microcontrollers had a rich instruction set. They are still studied in many academic courses. Philips contributed significantly to the embedded industry. Their legacy lives on in countless products today.

Philips Security Features

Philips implemented security features typical of the 8051 era. Their 80C51 devices have lock bits that, when set, prevent readback of the internal program memory. These bits can be activated during programming to protect the code. However, they can be bypassed using various techniques. For the LPC2000 series, Philips offered a security code that could be set to lock JTAG access. Without the correct code, you cannot read the memory. They also provided flash encryption for some parts. But these older protections are often vulnerable to glitch attacks. We can use voltage or clock glitches to temporarily unlock the device. Side‑channel analysis can reveal the security code during authentication. Our lab has successfully extracted firmware from P80C51 devices by decapsulating and probing the die. We have also exploited a known vulnerability in the bootloader of LPC2138 to dump the flash. Philips’ security is relatively weak by modern standards, making them easier to hack. We can clone the firmware by simply reading the flash if the lock bits are not set. If they are set, we use invasive methods to recover the data. We have developed custom tools to attack the protection mechanism. For OTP versions, we read the content using micro‑probing. We can also duplicate the firmware onto new devices for legacy replacement. Many Philips MCUs lack a unique identifier, so cloning is straightforward. Our services are essential for maintaining old equipment. We provide a full dump of the program and data memory. We also recover encryption keys if present. Philips’ security features are minimal, but we have the expertise to overcome them. Trust MikaTech for all your Philips reverse engineering needs.
