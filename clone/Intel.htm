About Intel

Intel Corporation is a pioneer in microprocessor and microcontroller design. Its early MCS‑51 (8051) architecture became the industry standard for embedded systems. Intel also developed the 16‑bit MCS‑96 family for more demanding applications. The company later moved into ARM‑based XScale processors and the Quark SoC for IoT. Intel’s microcontrollers are known for robust performance and extensive peripheral sets. They have been used in automotive, telecommunications, and consumer electronics. The 8051 core remains popular due to its simplicity and rich ecosystem. Intel’s embedded controllers often integrate flash memory, RAM, and analog interfaces. They provided development tools like the iC‑51 compiler and emulators. Intel also produced the 186, 286, 386, and 486 processors that later influenced embedded computing. However, Intel mostly exited the standalone MCU market, focusing on x86 and FPGA. Legacy Intel MCUs are still abundant in industrial machinery and medical equipment. Their 8051 variants feature multiple package options and temperature ranges. Intel’s design philosophy emphasized reliability and ease of use. They offered extensive application notes and reference designs. Many engineers learned programming on Intel 8051 devices. The company also ventured into secure microcontrollers for banking and identification. Intel’s security co‑processors were used in trusted platform modules (TPMs). Their flash technology allowed in‑system programming and updates. Intel also produced EPROM and OTP versions for cost‑sensitive designs. Their microcontrollers supported multiple interrupt sources and timers. The MCS‑96 added advanced math capabilities and PWM outputs. Intel’s Quark series aimed at wearable and edge computing. Although no longer in the MCU business, their legacy parts are still manufactured by third parties. Intel’s influence on the industry is undeniable. Their documentation remains a valuable resource for reverse engineers. The company’s historical contributions include the first commercial microprocessor (4004). Intel’s 8051 derivatives have been cloned by many vendors. Their rich instruction set is well‑understood and widely supported. Even today, new designs occasionally use Intel‑compatible cores. Intel also produced the 80186 and 80286 embedded versions. Their microcontrollers have been employed in aerospace and defense. Intel’s strong brand and quality control made them a trusted supplier.

Intel Security Features

Intel implemented various security mechanisms over the years to protect firmware and data. Early 8051 devices had lock bits that could be programmed to prevent external readback. These lock bits, when set, blocked memory read instructions from the debug interface. However, glitch attacks can sometimes disable these locks temporarily. Intel also offered encrypted OTP variants where the contents were scrambled during programming. To decrypt the firmware, one would need to know the encryption key. Some MCS‑96 devices include a security password to unlock debug access. Without the correct password, you cannot extract the code. Intel introduced memory scrambling in later designs to hinder simple dumping. They also added hardware breakpoints and trace buffers for debugging, which could be abused to capture code execution. For secure applications, Intel recommended using external security chips. The Quark series had secure boot and measured launch to verify software integrity. These features make it harder to hack into the system. Intel’s TPM‑capable controllers store cryptographic keys in tamper‑resistant memory. Despite these protections, many legacy devices can be cloned by decapsulating and reading the die with a microscope. Fault injection via voltage or clock glitching remains effective against older Intel parts. Side‑channel power analysis can reveal secrets during encryption operations. Our team has successfully recovered firmware from 87C51 devices using differential power analysis. We have also used focused ion beam (FIB) to cut security fuses and unlock the memory. Intel’s older OTP parts are non‑reprogrammable, so we often dump them and duplicate onto new parts. For Quark processors, we extract the bootloader from SPI flash and then analyze the x86 code. Intel’s lack of strong authentication in some series allows researchers to read out the full ROM through JTAG. We have developed custom tools to attack the protection bits. By exploiting timing variances, we can infer secret data. Intel’s security is dated, but many critical systems still rely on these chips.
