About National Semiconductor

National Semiconductor was a pioneering semiconductor company known for its analog and microcontroller products. Their COP8 family of 8‑bit MCUs was widely used in automotive and industrial applications. National also produced the SC/MP (Simple Cost‑effective MicroProcessor) in the 1970s. They later introduced ARM‑based processors for embedded systems. National’s microcontrollers were known for low power and high reliability. They were used in engine control, medical monitors, and consumer electronics. National also offered a range of development tools and application notes. Their products were manufactured using advanced CMOS processes. National’s COP8 series featured integrated EEPROM and analog peripherals. They supported multiple communication protocols (SPI, I2C, UART). National was acquired by Texas Instruments in 2011, but many legacy chips remain in use. Their SC/MP architecture was one of the first microprocessors with built‑in I/O. National also produced the 32000 series of 32‑bit microprocessors. Their microcontrollers often included watchdog timers and brown‑out detectors. National’s devices were designed for harsh environments. They had a strong presence in the automotive industry. National provided extensive technical support and reference designs. Their COP8 devices are still found in industrial automation. National also offered radiation‑hardened versions for aerospace. Their legacy products are often subject to reverse engineering for maintenance and upgrades. National’s documentation is thorough and accessible. Their microcontrollers are well‑studied, making extraction feasible. National’s influence on the industry is still felt today. Many engineers were trained on National platforms. Their chips are known for long availability. National’s focus on analog integration made them unique. They were a key supplier to major OEMs. National’s products are still supported by third‑party suppliers.

National Semiconductor Security Features

National implemented basic security features in their COP8 series. These include a security bit that prevents readback of the ROM and EEPROM. When set, the chip cannot be read via the serial programming interface. However, this lock can be bypassed by applying a high voltage to the test pin. Glitch attacks on the clock signal can also unlock the device. Our lab has successfully dumped COP8 firmware using these techniques. The SC/MP had no security, making it trivial to read. Later ARM‑based parts adopted more advanced security, including secure boot and encryption. National’s security was sufficient for the era but is weak by today's standards. Many legacy systems rely on these chips, and their firmware is often lost. We recover data from National devices regularly. The process involves a combination of voltage manipulation and timing analysis. Once unlocked, we can read the entire memory and clone it onto a modern device. National’s devices are easy to duplicate. We provide the extracted code in binary and assembly formats. Our methods are non‑invasive, preserving the original chip. For COP8 OTP parts, we use decapsulation to read the fuse states. We can recover data even from partially damaged chips. National’s security is not designed to resist determined attackers. We have developed automated tools for mass extraction. Our team can handle high‑volume projects. We also offer analysis of the recovered code to understand its logic.
